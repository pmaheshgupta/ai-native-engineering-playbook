The evidence

AI speeds up some work a lot, and slows other work down

Teams often plan around one headline number, such as "20–30% more productive". The research doesn't support a single number. In controlled studies, AI made developers 56% faster on a small, new task and 19% slower on complex changes to code they already knew. Where you land depends on the kind of work you do. Most fintech production work is the second kind, so a realistic plan assumes modest gains until you have measured your own.

How to read these numbers

In the first group, each percentage compares how long the same kind of task took with and without AI. +56% means it got done 56% faster; −19% means it took 19% longer. The small bar shows where each result sits between 20% slower and 60% faster, with the line marking no change. The second group measures adoption and trust, not speed.

Speed: the same work, with and without AI

  • +56%

    Small, new, self-contained task

    GitHub's controlled trial: developers built an HTTP server in 1 hour 11 minutes with Copilot, against 2 hours 41 minutes without it.

    What it tells you: AI is strongest on greenfield work with clear boundaries.

    Peng et al., 2023 ↗
  • +42%

    Six-week rollout on real work at a bank

    ANZ Bank compared engineers with and without Copilot on their day-to-day tasks. Beginners gained 52%, senior engineers 40%.

    What it tells you: The gains are real in production, and larger for less experienced engineers.

    ANZ Bank study, 2024 ↗
  • −19%

    Complex changes in a mature codebase the developers knew well

    METR's controlled trial with experienced open-source maintainers. They expected AI to make them 24% faster and afterwards felt 20% faster, but the tasks took 19% longer.

    What it tells you: On hard domain work, AI can cost time, and people cannot tell from the inside.

    Becker et al., METR, 2025 ↗

Adoption and trust

  • 33%

    AI suggestions kept by developers

    Across more than 400 developers at ZoomInfo, engineers accepted about one in three AI suggestions. It struggled most with domain-specific logic.

    What it tells you: The work that makes your product different benefits least.

    ZoomInfo, 2025 ↗
  • 84% · 46%

    Developers using AI, and developers distrusting it

    Stack Overflow's 2025 survey: 84% use or plan to use AI tools, 46% distrust the accuracy of the output, and positive sentiment fell from over 70% to 60%.

    What it tells you: Adoption is running ahead of evidence, so usage alone proves nothing.

    Stack Overflow Developer Survey 2025 ↗

What to do with this

Your gain depends on your mix of work.

More boilerplate, tests and new code moves you toward +56%. More complex logic in mature code, which is most fintech production work, moves you toward zero or below.

Before believing any headline number, look at what kind of work your team actually does.

Engineers can't judge their own speed-up.

METR's developers were 19% slower while feeling 20% faster.

Don't justify AI spend with surveys. Measure cycle time, escaped defects and review load.

Does it pay off?

Building the Level 2 platform pays back only above about 15 engineers

Moving from AI-Assisted to AI-Augmented means building shared machinery: AI review on every pull request, shared context files, policy gates and metrics. That build costs roughly the same for 5 engineers as for 50, while the benefit grows with every engineer who uses it. So below a certain team size it never pays for itself. With a cautious 10% gain, that point is about 15 engineers.

Why the higher rungs pull away

L1Nothing compoundsA one-time individual speed-up, then flat.
L2Shared prompts, context, legible code, measurementEvery future AI interaction gets better.
L3Redesigned workflows and team skillAgents' output, which depends on good specs, tests and legible code.
L4The system improves itselfCapacity, with compute, and the learning rate.

L1 gains are a step you take once. From L2 up, gains accumulate, so the gap between a compounding org and a flat one widens at an accelerating rate. "We already get 20–30%, that's enough" is a decision to let competitors compound past you.
