The evidence · updated October 2026

AI pays off on some work, barely on other work, and only with the right system around it

By 2026 almost every engineering team uses AI, so the question has moved from "should we?" to "is it paying off?". The latest research answers clearly: it depends on the work and on the system around the tools. Measured gains average 15–20%. They reach 30–40% on simple new code and fall to 0–10% on complex work in large existing codebases. Teams that use more AI ship more, but they also break more. Most fintech production work is the complex, existing-code kind, so a realistic plan assumes modest gains, budgets for an early dip, and strengthens testing and review before scaling up.

How to read these numbers

Each line is one finding. The number on the left is the headline result, and the small label under it says what was measured, because these studies measure different things: delivered output, task time, self-reports and survey answers. In the speed group, the bar shows where each result sits between 20% slower and 60% faster, with the line marking no change.

Adoption: AI is now how teams work

  • 73%of AI-tool users use them daily

    AI is now the default way of working

    Stack Overflow's 2026 survey of more than 30,000 developers: 73% of those using AI coding tools use them every day, and 31% of daily users spend four or more hours a day with them. Two in three now work inside coding agents. Trust is conditional: 48% trust AI only when they can easily check its answer.

    What it tells you: Whether teams use AI is no longer the question. Whether they can verify its output is.

    Stack Overflow, Oct 2026 ↗
  • 90%use AI at work

    More AI means more throughput, and more instability

    DORA's March 2026 research: 90% of technology professionals use AI at work and over 80% believe it has made them more productive. Teams that use more AI ship more, but their delivery is also less stable, and 30% of developers have little or no confidence in AI-generated code.

    What it tells you: AI amplifies what is already there. Strong engineering foundations turn it into gains; weak ones turn it into failures.

    DORA, Mar 2026 ↗

Speed: it depends on the work

  • +30–40%net gain · simple, new code

    Simple tasks in new codebases

    Stanford's study of about 100,000 developers at more than 600 companies measured delivered functionality, not commits or lines of code, and subtracted the rework AI caused. Simple work on new code gained 30–40%.

    What it tells you: AI is strongest on greenfield work with clear boundaries.

    Stanford, Denisov-Blanch ↗
  • 0–10%net gain · complex, existing code

    Complex tasks in large, existing codebases

    In the same study, complex work in mature codebases gained only 0–10%, and gains shrank as codebases grew. Averaged across all work, the net gain was 15–20%. DORA's 2026 report cites the same pattern.

    What it tells you: Most fintech production work looks like this. Plan for the low end until you have measured your own.

    Stanford, Denisov-Blanch ↗ · InfoQ summary ↗
  • 19% slowerearly 2025 → faster by 2026

    Experienced developers on their own projects

    METR's 2025 trial found experienced open-source developers took 19% longer with AI. Its February 2026 follow-up suggests they are now faster, but METR calls that weak evidence: so many developers now refuse to work without AI that a fair comparison is hard to run.

    What it tells you: The tools improved fast, and controlled trials can't keep up. You need your own before-and-after numbers.

    METR, 2025 trial ↗ · METR, Feb 2026 ↗

Perception and payback

  • 3×self-reported speed-up

    What people say AI does for them

    METR's 2026 survey of 349 technical workers: the median respondent said AI makes them 3× faster and their work 1.4–2× more valuable. METR cautions that self-reports run well ahead of measurements; in its 2025 trial, people misjudged AI's effect on their time by about 40 percentage points.

    What it tells you: Self-reported 3× against a measured 15–20%: never justify AI spend on how much faster people feel.

    METR, May 2026 ↗
  • 5% → 6%change-failure rate in DORA's example

    Returns come after a dip, and from the system around the tools

    DORA's 2026 ROI report finds productivity usually dips first, while teams learn, review load grows and testing and approvals catch up. In its worked example for a 500-person organization, a rise in change-failure rate from 5% to 6% erases about $344,000 of value.

    What it tells you: Budget for the dip, and fund testing, CI and code review before you scale AI use.

    DORA ROI report, 2026 ↗ · InfoQ summary ↗

What to do with this

Your gain depends on your mix of work.

Simple work on new code moves you toward 30–40%. Complex changes in large existing codebases, which is most fintech production work, move you toward 0–10%.

Before believing any headline number, look at what kind of work your team actually does.

Engineers can't judge their own speed-up.

People report AI makes them 3× faster; measured net gains average 15–20%.

Don't justify AI spend with surveys. Measure cycle time, change-failure rate and review load before and after.

Does it pay off?

Building the Level 2 platform pays back only above about 15 engineers

Moving from AI-Assisted to AI-Augmented means building shared machinery: AI review on every pull request, shared context files, policy gates and metrics. That build costs roughly the same for 5 engineers as for 50, while the benefit grows with every engineer who uses it. So below a certain team size it never pays for itself. With a cautious 10% gain, that point is about 15 engineers.

Why the higher rungs pull away

L1Nothing compoundsA one-time individual speed-up, then flat.
L2Shared prompts, context, legible code, measurementEvery future AI interaction gets better.
L3Redesigned workflows and team skillAgents' output, which depends on good specs, tests and legible code.
L4The system improves itselfCapacity, with compute, and the learning rate.

L1 gains are a step you take once. From L2 up, gains accumulate, so the gap between a compounding org and a flat one widens at an accelerating rate. "We already get 20–30%, that's enough" is a decision to let competitors compound past you.
