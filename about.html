About

Hi, I'm Mahesh.

I live in Hyderabad and build software for finance, where a bug can mean lost money and any decision might one day have to be explained to a regulator. That shapes how I think about AI. I'm genuinely excited by how much of the work it can take on, and I always want to know who is accountable when it does.

Why I wrote this

Most conversations about AI in engineering stop at "our developers are 20–30% faster". That number rarely survives contact with real fintech codebases, and it doesn't tell a leader what to do on Monday. I wanted something more useful: where your team actually is, whether climbing the next rung pays off at your size, and what to build first, with the brakes funded as fast as the engine.

The problem I keep coming back to

Rules locked inside documents. Pricing rules, fund agreements, regulations: people write them in prose, and someone has to turn them into something a system can execute and prove is right. I first chased it by hand-building a compiler for a rules language. Now I chase it with agents. The tools changed; the question didn't. How do you turn human intent into machine action and still trust the result? This playbook is that same question, asked about the whole engineering organization.

What I believe

  • Humans move up, not out.AI-first means people spend their time on judgment, specification and verification.
  • Measure, don't feel.Developers can feel 20% faster while being 19% slower. Cycle time and failure rates settle the argument.
  • Brakes before engine.Rollback, enforced gates and named owners come before more autonomy.
  • Standing still can be right.For a small team, staying at AI-Assisted is a disciplined choice, not a failure.
  • Compliance can be a growth lever.Done well, the guardrails shorten security reviews and become a moat.

If your team is working out its own climb, I'm always happy to compare notes.
